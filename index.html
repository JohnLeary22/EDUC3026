A NOTE BEFORE DRAWING CONCLUSIONS

A simulation is not the student's experience.

Simulated impairment can unintentionally shape teachers' judgements—encouraging fear, pity or assumptions of lower capability. Use it to notice possible barriers, not to decide what a student can do.

LIVED EXPERIENCE · 2022

Maher, Haegele & Sparkes examine how people with visual impairment view the ethics of sighted teachers simulating impairment.

Read the study ↗
CAPABILITY JUDGEMENTS · 2015

Silverman, Gwinn & Van Boven found that people who simulated blindness judged blind people as less capable than those who did not.

Read the research ↗

Keep the balance: simulation + first-person stories + examples of successful adaptations. Never let a simulation stand in for listening.

02 / LISTEN FIRSTACCESS · COMMUNICATE

C COMMUNICATE

Make it a dialogue, not an interview.

Consult with the student, caregivers and relevant teachers or support staff. Avoid the pattern of asking questions and then making decisions without the student. Agree how everyone can contribute, and give the student genuine influence over goals and strategies.

04 / BELONGING IN ACTIONACCESS · ENGAGE

E ENGAGE

Participation is the point.

This is the day-to-day work of inclusion, and often where teachers spend most of the time. Ask whether the student can participate meaningfully—not only whether information reaches them. Keep the focus on strengths, interests, agency and belonging.

“

Plan for the student to take part with peers. Do not confuse being present, quiet or receiving instructions with meaningful inclusion.

QUICK FORMATIVE CHECK

What would you do?

Choose a response in each classroom scenario to reveal feedback.

01

Reading the whiteboard

A Year 7 student with low vision is squinting at the board and copying notes much more slowly than everyone else.

In betweenIt might help, but sitting closer doesn't always improve vision and could make the student uncomfortable.

Good responseThis lets the student access the same information without relying on their ability to see the board.

Poor responseThe student is still missing information during the lesson and becomes reliant on someone else to catch up.

02

Understanding a diagram

During a Geography lesson, a student with vision impairment struggles to follow a detailed map projected onto the screen.

Poor responseThis removes part of the learning activity instead of making it accessible, so the student misses out.

In betweenPeer support can help, but relying on classmates every time isn't a proper long-term adjustment.

Good responseThe student can follow the explanation alongside everyone else, rather than missing the visual parts of the lesson.

03

Glare from the windows

A student with light sensitivity keeps shielding their eyes because sunlight is reflecting off the desks near the windows.

Good responseIt addresses the actual problem while letting the student have some say in what works for them.

Poor responseThey lose learning time because of something the teacher could have addressed with a fairly simple adjustment.

In betweenThis might reduce glare, but could make visibility worse for the student and everyone else in the room.

04

Science practical

During a science practical, a student with vision impairment is having trouble identifying measurements on the equipment.

In betweenThey still participate in some learning, but miss the hands-on experience that everyone else gets.

Poor responseThe teacher assumes the student cannot participate without properly considering adjustments or assessing the actual risks.

Good responseThis keeps the student involved in the practical task while addressing any specific safety concerns.

05

Moving between classrooms

A student with limited peripheral vision is unsure how to get to a new classroom after a timetable change.

Poor responseThis ignores possible hazards and assumes following other students is enough to navigate an unfamiliar space safely.

Good responseClear directions help the student navigate independently without assuming they need someone guiding them everywhere.

In betweenIt may help initially, but could create unnecessary dependence and put responsibility onto another student.

06

Completing a worksheet

A student with low vision is struggling to read the small text and crowded layout on a worksheet.

Good responseThe student can complete the same work independently using tools that suit their particular vision needs.

In betweenLarger text might help, but not every impairment benefits from enlargement, and it may change the layout.

Poor responseThis takes away the student's chance to work through the questions themselves and demonstrate their understanding.

05 / LEARN AND ADAPTACCESS · SEE

S SEE WHAT WORKED

Let the student's VOICE lead the review.

Academic progress matters, but it is only one part of success. Review the adjustment with the student and consider access, opportunity, independence, learning and the student's experience together.

V

Voice

What did the student say about the strategy? Did they feel listened to and able to raise concerns?

O

Opportunity

Did it increase opportunities to participate, contribute and connect with peers?

I

Independence

Could the student act with greater autonomy and choose when support was useful?

C

Curriculum

Could the student access the intended learning and demonstrate what they know?

E

Experience

Did comfort, confidence, engagement or sense of belonging improve?

Use VOICE as a practical reflection guide, not a score or a substitute for the student's own account. Adapt questions and response formats so the student can share comfortably.

This page does not save notes. Follow your school's privacy and record-keeping practices when documenting student information.

06 / KEEP ACCESS GOINGACCESS · SUPPORT

S SUPPORT ONGOING ACCESS

Maintain. Adapt. Restart.

Successful adjustments should not become static adjustments. Needs, subjects, environments and student preferences change. ACCESS is a cycle: return to Assess when something changes, and begin again with the student.

01

Maintain

Continue strategies that work. Keep them documented, prepared and available.

02

Adapt

Adjust when the learner, task, environment or preference changes. Review together.

03

Restart

Return to A—Assess the experience and barriers—and repeat the ACCESS cycle.

Times to begin the cycle again

  1. Beginning of a new term or subject
  2. Change of classroom or teacher
  3. Introduction of new technology
  4. A new assessment type
  5. An excursion or practical activity
  6. The student raises a concern
  7. An adjustment stops working
  8. Functional vision or specialist recommendations change
FOR THE TEACHER, TOO

Make the next step manageable.

It can feel stressful to start or revisit adjustments. You do not need to solve everything at once: choose one priority with the student, use your school's support team and existing plans, agree who can help, and set a date to check in.

ACCESS IS A CYCLE, NOT A CHECKLIST

Maintain what works. Adapt when things change. Restart with the student's voice at the centre.
